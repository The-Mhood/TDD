# TDD — Explorer's Report to the Queen

**Document ID:** TDD-IMPL-WORLD-009 · **Version:** 1.0 · **Date:** 2026-11-21
**Companion to:** `WORLD_SCALE_AND_LIVING_WORLD_BLUEPRINT.md` (TDD-IMPL-WORLD-001)
**Author:** PM (literary world document). **Voice:** a first-person expedition journal, written by an envoy of the Queen to map and report the Ọmà Ala.
**Note on names & culture:** Igbo names and terms throughout. Terms are glossed at the end. Sacred places are described with restraint — access restrictions are recorded but their inner workings are not invented. The Ancestral Realm is referenced only as its established narrative space.

## 1. The commission

Your Majesty, you sent me to look upon the land of the Ọmà and to return with its shape and its people, so that your eye may travel where your feet cannot yet go. I took the path at the dawn of Ọnwa Mbụ, when the first rains had softened the earth and the river was beginning to rise. This is my report of the Ọmà Ala — the land of the Ọmà — as I found it.

## 2. The first sight of the river

On the second day I came over a low ridge and saw the Ọmà. It is not a river that whispers; it is a river that speaks, wide and brown and moving with a great steady strength, carrying the canoes of the fisher-folk and the goods of the traders north and south. The people of the Ọmà Ala call the river their spine, and so it is. No town or village sits far from its voice.

## 3. The towns upon the water

I came first to **Ọmà Ukwu**, the big town on the Ọmà's bank. It is the heart of trade in this land. Its market square is vast, and on Eke day it swells with the whole region — yam and palm and fish and iron and cloth, all laid out in heaps under the open sky. The town has a craft quarter where smiths and woodworkers keep their fires, and a dock where canoes are tied in a long row. It is the busiest place I have seen in the Ọmà Ala.

## 4. The hill town of Ịbe

Travelling east, the land rises to the **Ahioma** hills, and here sits **Ịbe**. The people of Ịbe are hill-folk and smiths. Their iron is sought across the Ọmà Ala — hoes for the farms, knives for the kitchens, gongs for the squares. On the ridge I could see the whole valley at once: the river thread, the farmland, the green of the forest. From a hill one sees how the world is laid out, and how small each village is beside it.

## 5. The villages along the water

Below Ọmà Ukwu, the Ọmà runs between farmland, and here are the villages — **Ahanwụ**, **Ihiala**, **Ụkwụ**, **Ezimmiri**. Each has its own manner. Ahanwụ is a farming village of compounds set among yam and palm. Ihiala sits between the Ọmà and the Ụmụ and is rich in fish and palm. Ụkwụ is a river-village, its people living with the water. Ezimmiri, at the forest edge, is a place of potters, their clay drawn from the pits where the forest begins.

## 6. The compounds and the obi

A home in the Ọmà Ala is not a single house but a **compound** — an **ezi** — a cluster of huts set around an open space, walled with red earth. At the gate-side stands the **obi**, the head-of-house's hut, where the family receives its guests and settles its matters. Behind it are the huts of the wives and children, the barn where yams are hung, the shed for goats, the little shrine-grove where the family keeps its own. To enter a compound is to enter a whole household, and everywhere I was received with the courtesy the land demands.

## 7. The Ụmụ and the Nkwụ

The Ọmà has two sisters that join it. To the east, the **Ụmụ River** runs down to meet it, and Ihiala and Ụkwụ sit upon it. To the west, the **Nkwụ Stream** waters the reed-beds and the wetland where the raffia grows. The two tributaries are the region's other arteries — fish and reed and palm flow along them as the Ọmà flows.

## 8. The market days

Hear me, Your Majesty, on the market days, for they are the pulse of this land. The week here has **four days** — **Eke, Orie, Afo, Nkwo** — and every settlement keeps its market upon its own day. On Eke the great market of Ọmà Ukwu is full; on Orie the fish-market of **Ọnịcha** and the hill-market of Ịbe are busy; on Afo the cassava and grain market of **Ọba** spreads its stalls. No day passes in all the Ọmà Ala without some market somewhere being held. One travels to the market that is full on the day one arrives, and so the choice of where to go is the choice of which market to find.

## 9. The market in the rain

I was in Ọmà Ukwu on an Eke that rained hard, and I tell you: a market in rain is not the same market as a market in dry sun. The traders drew their cloths and reed-mats over the goods; the ground became mud, and the buyers came fewer but more determined, stepping carefully, bargaining under cover. On a dry Eke the same square is a wide blaze of people and colour. The land does not look the same on every visit, and neither does its market.

## 10. The seasons

There are two seasons in the Ọmà Ala, not four. There is the **rain**, which fills the rivers and the floodplain and turns the paths to mud and makes the forest grow thick and green; and there is the **dry**, when the water falls and the land dries and the harvest is gathered. The yam is the lord of this calendar. When the rains end, the yams are lifted, and the land holds its great **Iri Ji** — the new-yam festival — with feasting and dancing. I have not seen a European four-season here; there is only wet and dry, and each governs everything.

## 11. The fields and the yam

The yam is the honour of this people. A man's standing is read in his yam-barn. The fields lie in terraces and plots through the valley, intercropped with cassava and cocoyam and palm, and the farm-work follows the rain. In the rain the land is planted and weeded; in the dry it is harvested and stored. The fields are not empty between seasons — they are the work of the whole year, and the farmers are out in them from first light.

## 12. The forest and its giving

At the southwest lies the **Ezi-na-Oke**, the dense forest. It is a place of giving and a place of warning. Here the woodworkers take their timber, the hunters take their game, the herbalists take their medicines, and the honey-men take their honey. But it is not a gentle place; a traveller who leaves the path in the deep forest does not lightly return. The forest is the world's store-room and its danger, and the people know both.

## 13. The craft of the hands

I learned that no town in the Ọmà Ala makes all things for itself, and this is the strength of the land. Ịbe makes iron; Ezimmiri makes pots; Ọnịcha weaves mats and cloth; Ụkwụ dries fish. The yam of the farm is carried to the towns, the iron of the hill is carried to the farms, the pots and cloth move everywhere. Each place is known by what its hands do, and the regions hold each other up.

## 14. The council and the square

When I asked who rules the Ọmà Ala, the people did not point to a single king. Each village has its **ama** — the square — and there the elders and the title-holders gather to speak and to settle. A man's standing is in his **ụmụnna**, his kindred, the sons of one father. There is no single throne over all the Ọmà Ala; the land is held village by village, and the decisions are taken by those who sit in the square. This is the way of the Igbo, and it is the way here.

## 15. The shrine-groves and their bounds

I will speak plainly and with restraint, Your Majesty. Each village keeps its **shrine-grove**, and there are places on the Ahioma ridge held sacred, with their own bounds and rules. I did not enter them, and I did not ask what is done within, for these are the people's own, and the bounds are theirs to keep. I record only that they exist and that they are respected — that some places are set apart and not freely entered. I have not invented their rites, and I report them no further than this.

## 16. The road and the journey

I measured the journeys as I went. From Ahanwụ to Ọmà Ukwu is a walk of some four kilometres — an hour or a little more. From Ọmà Ukwu to Ịbe, north along the water, is near a full morning. The cross of the whole region is a walk of two or three hours at the least, and the river adds the canoe journey. No place is two minutes from another. One travels to reach the market, to visit a kinsman, to buy the iron or the pot, and the road itself is full of the people going to their own business — farmers with loads, traders with carts, hunters with their dogs.

## 17. The people

The people are not a picture. They are farmers and traders and smiths and potters and weavers and fishers and hunters and builders; they are children at play in the square and elders who hold the council; they are the head-of-house in his **obi** receiving a guest and the traveller resting at a trader's hostel. They have homes they return to, work they go to, and places they are bound for. A village is alive, not staged, and this is what I most wished to bring back to you.

## 18. The things I could not reach

There are places I did not enter and could not fully report: the deep forest beyond the paths, the high ridge in the far east, and that which the people speak of only in the language of memory and spirit — the **Ancestral Realm**, which is not an ordinary place of the Ọmà Ala but a narrative space, kept as the people hold it. I did not press where I was not given, and I do not pretend to have mapped what I did not see. A report is honest about its own bounds.

## 19. Conclusion and the shape of the land

So, Your Majesty, I return with this shape: one great river, the Ọmà, with its sisters the Ụmụ and the Nkwụ; two towns, Ọmà Ukwu and Ịbe; the market towns of Ọnịcha and Ọba; the villages of Ahanwụ, Ihiala, Ụkwụ and Ezimmiri; the farmland, the forest, the floodplain and the hills; four market days turning in their cycle; two seasons, rain and dry; and a people whose lives run through all of it. The Ọmà Ala is not one village but a whole land, and it is alive. This is what I have to give you — the world, as I found it, drawn true.
